A server-sent events stream ends two ways

Server-sent events are the least fashionable way to push something to a browser and usually the right one. One long-lived response, lines of text, and EventSource reconnects on its own. No handshake, no second protocol, no library.

The part that goes wrong is not the sending. It is the stopping.

The version everyone writes first

${html(s.naive)}

Open a stream, subscribe to whatever changes, and unsubscribe when the request aborts. That reads as complete. It is not.

There are two endings, and they are different events

The socket going away fires abort on the request signal. That is the one everybody handles: the reader closed the tab, the network dropped, the process was told to stop.

The other one comes from the other side. When whoever is reading the stream lets go of the reader, the stream's own cancel runs.

${html(s.cancel)}

Nothing fires abort in that case. The socket is fine. The consumer stopped consuming, and that happens more often than it sounds. A runtime tears down a response. A proxy in front of you gives up. Your own code calls cancel on a reader it no longer needs.

What each half costs on its own

Handle only abort and a canceled stream leaves the subscription behind. Nothing errors. The listener stays on the list, holding whatever it closed over, and the next change calls it. On a server that runs for weeks this is the leak you find by watching memory rather than by reading a stack.

Handle only cancel and an aborted stream never unsubscribes, which is the same leak arriving by the other road. Handle both without noticing they can both happen, and you close a controller that is already closed, which throws.

The shape that survives both

${html(s.both)}

Three things are doing work there. One function ends the subscription, and a flag makes it idempotent, because either ending may come first and both may come at all. The send is guarded, because a change can arrive in the moment between the socket going and this hearing about it. And the close is wrapped, because the consumer may have closed the controller already.

None of that is defensive programming for its own sake. Each line is one of the two endings arriving when the other has already been dealt with.

The header nobody mentions

${html(s.headers)}

x-accel-buffering: no is the one that costs an afternoon. nginx buffers responses by default, which means it holds your lines until the response ends. For a stream that never ends, that is forever. It works perfectly on your machine and sends nothing in production.

What to send down it

A nudge, not the markup. Say that something changed and let the page ask for it on the URL that already returns it. Sending rendered HTML down the stream is a second way to render the same thing, and two ways to render one thing will drift.

Both endings above happened while this was being written. That is the only reason the code has a flag in it.
